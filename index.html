Publications

Perceived Central Bank Partisanship and the Anchoring of Inflation Expectations Forthcoming

with Changkeun Lee and Soomin Lim

Economics Letters

Working paper

Central banks aim to anchor inflation expectations by announcing targets, but doing so requires that the public find the announcement credible. Using an information-provision experiment with 2,000 Korean adults in a politically polarized but inflation-stable economy, we show that anchoring strengthens with the perceived political alignment between respondents and the central bank. The anchoring rate is about 10 pp higher at one year and 12 pp higher at five years for respondents who perceive a strong own-side lean than for those who perceive a strong opposing lean, against full-sample averages of 29% and 22%.

A Comparison of Korean and EU Competition Law on the Regulation of Information Exchange

with Hyeonjin Chon and Jeong Yeol Kim

The Korean Journal of Industrial Organization, 33(4), 2025 (in Korean)

Published version

The term “stand-alone information exchange” is used to describe an information exchange that is treated as a cartel in its own right. It may take various forms and, without leaving evidence of an agreement, can generate anti-competitive effects comparable to collusion. This study compares the Korean and EU competition law regimes that treat stand-alone information exchange as an unlawful concerted practice along three dimensions: undertakings, coordination and the anti-competitive effects of that coordination. Korea regulates it through a prescriptive, rule-based regime that requires an agreement to establish coordination and assesses effects largely by observed market outcomes. The EU relies on a principles-based notion of “concerted practice” that does not require proof of an agreement and assesses effects by the nature and purpose of the information exchanged. On this basis, the paper evaluates the Korean regulation in light of EU competition law.

How the FOMC Weighs Its Dual Mandate Under Review

PDF

We estimate the weight on unemployment relative to inflation in the Federal Reserve’s quadratic loss function over 2012–2020. Large language models count the negative and positive words in each member’s remarks in the FOMC transcripts. Taking net negativity as a proxy proportional to the loss, we regress it on the Tealbook forecasts. The results show that the FOMC places greater weight on the inflation gap than on the unemployment gap. A weight recovered from the observed federal funds rate target, using the FOMC’s projections in the Summary of Economic Projections together with the Board’s own models, EDO and LINVER, is consistent with the transcript estimate.

Simulating Analyst Forecast Behavior using LLMs

with Seohyun Lee

KDI School Working Paper No. 26-14

SSRN Slides

We examine whether a commercial large language model (LLM) can match human analysts in numerical earnings per share (EPS) forecasting. Using 121,369 forecasts for 607 Korean listed firms over 2018–2025, we generate paired LLM forecasts utilizing only information publicly available as of the analyst forecast date, with external search disabled. Human analysts remain more accurate on average: median forecast errors are 1.9% of stock price for analysts and 2.3% for the LLM, implying that the LLM underperforms analysts by about 20%. The LLM outperforms consensus forecasts but remains less accurate than analysts’ prior forecasts. The analyst advantage is larger for loss-making, highly leveraged, and smaller firms, as well as for firms with greater analyst disagreement. Comparing model generations under identical prompts, GPT-5.4-nano closes 68% of the gap between GPT-4.1-nano and human analysts.
